## Introduction

Randomization is the gold standard for causal inference in medicine. Treatment is assigned by chance, so measured and unmeasured characteristics are, on average, balanced between arms. Differences in outcomes can then be attributed to the treatment itself.^1^ Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are, however, expensive and time-consuming. They are also often infeasible or unrepresentative for important populations, such as older, multimorbid or underrepresented patients who receive these therapies in practice.^2^

Inferring causal effects from the data already generated in routine care therefore remains an unmet need. Target trial emulation (TTE), in which the protocol of a hypothetical RCT is specified and then emulated in observational data, is a leading candidate approach.^3^ Confounding is traditionally addressed with propensity scores (PS) estimated from structured variables chosen by investigators. This strategy balances measured characteristics well, and benchmarking against completed RCTs has shown that well-designed emulations can reproduce trial results.^4,5^ However, its guarantee extends only to what is measured. Most emulations rely on claims data, which record diagnoses, procedures and prescriptions consistently.^6^ Electronic health records (EHRs) capture clinical measurements at the point of care and are available within individual health systems, making them actionable for site-specific evidence generation.^7^ However, laboratory values, vital signs and imaging are missing for many patients, and not at random. These measurements therefore cannot be relied on for adjustment.^8^

Learned representations offer a different route to adjustment. Instead of an investigator choosing covariates, a model trained on large volumes of routine data compresses each patient's information into a general-purpose numerical summary, which can then be added to a PS. Two kinds of representation are available for most patients in an EHR. The first is physiological. The 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) is inexpensive and acquired routinely across the health system,^9^ and artificial intelligence applied to the ECG (AI-ECG) detects left ventricular dysfunction, structural heart disease and other latent phenotypes.^9–12^ Foundation-model embeddings of the ECG compress this information into representations of cardiac physiology.^13^ The second is a representation of the coded record. EHR foundation models such as CLMBR-T are trained on sequences of diagnosis, medication, procedure, measurement and visit codes, and produce a summary of each patient's coded history that reflects the sequence of events and, potentially, their timing.^22,23^ The two differ in what they can see: the ECG records current cardiac physiology, whereas a coded-record model sees which conditions were diagnosed and which tests and treatments were ordered, and in what sequence, but not the measured values.

Whether either kind of representation improves confounding control beyond hand-built PS covariates is not known. In particular, it is unclear whether a learned representation of the coded record adds anything beyond a simple list of the diagnoses a patient has had, or whether its advantage, if any, reflects information such as the timing of diagnoses relative to the treatment decision.

Here, in Trial Replication and Confounding Evaluation using ECG (TRACE-ECG), we compared two learned representations, an AI-ECG embedding and CLMBR-T, with a ladder of hand-built PS specifications in 32 emulations of cardiovascular RCTs. Taking a PS of demographics and five cardiovascular diagnoses as the primary comparison, we assessed (i) balance on held-out clinical, laboratory and echocardiographic characteristics, (ii) whether CLMBR-T improves on a PS of yes/no flags for every recorded diagnosis, (iii) agreement with RCT results across every matching and weighting configuration, (iv) which kinds of trial benefit, and (v) bias removal in simulations with a known treatment effect.

## Methods

### Data sources

We used EHR data from the Yale New Haven Health System (YNHHS), a large academic health system in the American northeast. We mapped the structured EHR data to the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) common data model. We linked these data to structured echocardiography reports, raw 12-lead ECG signals and state vital statistics records (eMethods 1). We included patients with index dates from 2011 through 2024. The Yale Institutional Review Board approved the study and waived informed consent for this secondary analysis of existing data. For external replication, we used MIMIC-IV (version 3.1), deidentified EHR and 12-lead ECG data from patients treated at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston, Massachusetts (eMethods 11).^14^

### Trial selection and target trial specification

Trial selection followed our previous report (eMethods 3). Of 99 candidate cardiovascular RCTs, 38 met the feasibility criteria and were emulated, and 32 were retained after grading of emulation quality (eFigure 1 of the paper 1 supplement [CHECK: renumber when the paper 2 supplement is assembled]). For each trial, we specified the target trial protocol and its emulation following the TARGET guideline, summarized using the population, intervention, comparator, outcome and time (PICOT) elements (Table 1; eMethods 2).^15^ Each emulation used a new-user, active-comparator design and estimated the effect of treatment initiation, analogous to the intention-to-treat effect. Emulation quality was graded from design information alone, using a points-based adaptation of the RCT-DUPLICATE design-emulation criteria, as excellent, good, moderate or limited (eMethods 3).^5^ The 32 analysed trials (3 excellent, 12 good and 17 moderate emulations) formed the primary set [USER: confirm S32 as primary set]; all 38 emulated trials were analysed as a sensitivity set.

### Learned representations

*AI-ECG embedding.* We used the most recent 12-lead ECG within 365 days before or on the index date. ECGs were encoded with an AI-ECG signal model adapted from an image-based biometric contrastive learning (BCL) model, a self-supervised approach that learns patient-specific representations of cardiac structure and function without diagnostic labels.^13^ The model produced a 256-dimensional embedding per ECG, which was reduced to 32 principal components within each trial (eMethods 4).

*CLMBR-T.* CLMBR-T is a transformer foundation model of the coded EHR, pretrained elsewhere on the structured records of a large patient population [CHECK: CLMBR-T-base, 141 million parameters, pretrained on 2.57 million Stanford patients; from the pre-2026-10-06 eMethods 4].^22,23^ It is not a model of unstructured data. We applied it frozen to each patient's coded history: diagnoses, drug orders, procedures, measurement codes without their values, and visits. Events on the index day were excluded, so its input window ended on the day before index, and the most recent 4,096 tokens were used; truncation affected 0.3% to 5.7% of patients per trial. Codes for the trial's study drugs appeared in the pre-index history of at most 5.9% of patients. Its output representation [CHECK: 768 dimensions] was reduced to 64 principal components within each trial [USER: confirm 64 CLMBR-T PCs as primary], with the first 32 components as a sensitivity analysis matching the dimension of the ECG.

### Propensity score specifications

Within each trial, we estimated the PS by L2-penalized logistic regression on standardized covariates, with the same penalty (inverse regularization strength 1) for every specification, so that only the covariates differed between comparisons (eMethods 5). We compared a ladder of five hand-built specifications. PS-Demo included age, sex and calendar year of index. PS-CVD5, the primary comparison, added five cardiovascular and metabolic diagnoses (hypertension, type 2 diabetes, coronary artery disease, AF and heart failure). PS-Flags added to PS-Demo a yes/no flag for every ICD-10-CM 3-character diagnosis code recorded at any time before the index day with a prevalence of at least 1% in the trial cohort, giving 426 to 622 flags per trial (median, 529). Its window matched the CLMBR-T input window, and it carried no dates, counts or recency. The high-dimensional PS (hdPS) added 200 empirically selected code features to demographics and 9 to 13 investigator-selected prior diagnoses,^16^ and the clinical PS (PS-Clinical) further included vital signs, laboratory values, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), medications and healthcare use (eMethods 5).

Each specification was fitted alone (base) and with the ECG components, the CLMBR-T components, or both, denoted, for example, PS-CVD5 + ECG. As placebos, embeddings were permuted between patients within each trial (permuted ECG and permuted CLMBR-T), which preserves their dimension but removes their information. In the primary configuration, patients were matched 1:1 without replacement by nearest-neighbour matching on the PS logit with a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit. Every specification and arm was also run in five further configurations: a caliper of 0.1, 1:3 matching, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), overlap weighting^25^ and a gradient-boosted PS with 5-fold cross-fitting.

The PS-Demo + CLMBR-T vs PS-Flags comparison asks whether a learned summary of the coded record, which reflects the sequence of events, adds anything beyond yes/no flags for the same diagnoses; one hypothesis is that CLMBR-T captures the timing of diagnoses relative to the treatment decision, which flags do not. The comparison does not isolate timing, because CLMBR-T also sees drugs, procedures, measurement codes and visits, and no PS with diagnosis dates alone was fitted.

### Trial labels

Before any label-stratified result was computed, each trial was labelled for whether, in routine care, the choice between intervention and comparator is plausibly driven substantially by cardiac structure, function, rhythm or conduction, or haemodynamic or congestion state (physiologic, yes or no), and for the expected cardiac confounding domains (LV structure, LV systolic function, diastolic function and left atrium, RV and pulmonary congestion, rhythm and conduction, or none). The labels were produced by two separate runs of the same large language model (Claude), each blind to the other and to all emulation results, RCT estimates and quality grades, working from a results-free PICOT table. The two runs agreed on every physiologic label (κ = 1.00). Because both runs used the same model and prompt, this agreement is expected and is not evidence of inter-rater reliability. Final domains were those named by both runs, with the union as a sensitivity set. The model knows the published trials, so the labels are not independent of trial knowledge. No clinician has yet reviewed the labels [USER: confirm whether a clinician check of the 11 physiologic labels will be done]. Labels for all 38 trials are in Table 1.

### Covariate balance

The primary outcome was balance, after matching or weighting, on characteristics not included in the PS under evaluation, summarized as the absolute standardized mean difference (SMD).^17^ The primary panel comprised 54 non-coded characteristics: vital signs and core laboratory values including LVEF (9), other laboratory values including N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) (10), and 35 echocardiographic measures of LV structure, LV function, diastolic function and left atrial size, RV and pulmonary measures, and valves and aorta (eTable 2) [USER: confirm the 54-item panel as the primary balance endpoint]. It is the 58-item panel of our previous report without its 4 coded-record summaries (medications, healthcare use, held-out codes and a prognostic score), because CLMBR-T and PS-Flags draw on the codes behind those summaries; the 58-item panel is reported as secondary. At PS-Clinical, the characteristics included in that PS were also removed. "Held out" means that the value is not in the PS; diagnosis codes in PS-Flags, the hdPS and CLMBR-T can still act as proxies for some held-out values (for example, codes for obesity, kidney disease or heart failure).

### Trial emulation analysis

Emulated HRs were estimated with Cox proportional hazards models with robust variance clustered on matched pairs. Following the RCT-DUPLICATE initiative, agreement with each RCT was assessed as the Pearson correlation of emulated and RCT log HRs across trials; estimate agreement, defined as an emulated HR within the RCT 95% CI; and standardized difference agreement, defined as a difference between emulated and RCT log HRs within 1.96 times its combined standard error.^5,18^ We also used the absolute difference between emulated and RCT log HRs (|Δ log HR|). In the benchmark-permutation test, RCT results were reassigned at random across trials 20,000 times, to assess whether a reduction in |Δ log HR| was specific to each trial's own RCT result or reflected generic attenuation of extreme estimates (eMethods 6).

### Simulation

We extended the plasmode simulations of our previous report,^19^ which retain each trial's real covariates, ECGs and follow-up while simulating treatment and outcome with a known HR. We used its primary design, in which a withheld physiological variable (LVEF, NT-proBNP, body mass index or estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]) alone drove treatment and also affected the outcome, with LVEF and eGFR oriented clinically (lower values increasing treatment and hazard), an orientation chosen post hoc in the previous report (eMethods 7). We added PS-CVD5 alone and with the ECG, CLMBR-T or both, a PS of CLMBR-T alone and PS-CVD5 with permuted CLMBR-T, using the same cells, seeds and replicates. Existing arms were reproduced exactly before the new arms were run.

### External replication

In MIMIC-IV, we added a PS-CVD5 analog (demographics plus hypertension, type 2 diabetes, coronary artery disease, AF and heart failure coded in admissions before the index admission) to the previously reported analysis of 7 cardiovascular trials and a negative-control trial (PEPTIC), with and without the ECG and the permuted ECG. CLMBR-T is not available in MIMIC-IV. The held-out panel comprised 26 laboratory, vital sign, ventilation and utilization characteristics (eMethods 11).

### Statistical analysis

Trials were the unit of analysis. For each trial, balance was summarized as the mean |SMD| over the characteristics observed in both compared arms and as the proportion of characteristics with an |SMD| <0.1. The primary measure was the relative reduction in mean |SMD|, 1 minus the ratio of the across-trial means with and without the added representation, with 95% CIs from 4,000 bootstrap resamples of trials with a fixed seed. Paired differences were tested with exact sign-flip permutation tests across trials, one-sided for an embedding against its base or its placebo and two-sided for head-to-head comparisons between PS models (for example, CLMBR-T vs ECG, PS-Demo + CLMBR-T vs PS-Flags, and comparisons with the hdPS and PS-Clinical) and for placebos against base. Robustness was assessed by clustering trials that shared a comparator and by leave-one-trial-out analysis. The false discovery rate was controlled by the Benjamini–Hochberg method^24^ within trial set across all 337 contrast–configuration rows (every contrast in every configuration), separately for the balance family (mean |SMD| and proportion <0.1, over 54 and 58 characteristics) and the agreement family (|Δ log HR|, standardized difference agreement and the benchmark-permutation test). Domain-level results formed a separate exploratory family.

Three sensitivity analyses addressed the fairness of the PS-Flags comparison and the lower matching retention of PS models that included CLMBR-T. First, PS-Flags was refitted with its penalty chosen per trial by 5-fold cross-validated log loss (grid 10^−4^ to 10), and with fixed inverse regularization strengths of 0.1 and 0.01. Second, the head-to-head was restricted to trials in which PS-Flags retained at least 50%, 70% or 90% of the smaller treatment group. Third, the CLMBR-T gain was related to its loss of retention across trials, recomputed in trials with little loss, and compared with overlap weighting, which excludes no patients. These analyses are outside the FDR sweep.

Label analyses compared the gain from each representation at PS-CVD5 between labelled and unlabelled trials. The test statistic was the difference in the mean absolute gain in mean |SMD|, with two-sided P values from 10,000 permutations of labels across trials and Benjamini–Hochberg q values within label families. For domain labels, gains were computed over the held-out characteristics mapped to each domain (LV systolic function: echocardiographic LV function and LVEF; diastolic function and left atrium: the corresponding echocardiographic measures; RV and pulmonary congestion: RV and pulmonary measures and NT-proBNP). LV structure had only one labelled trial and was not tested, and the held-out panel has no rhythm or conduction measures. As a data-driven check, we correlated each trial's gain with its residual echocardiographic imbalance under PS-CVD5, using the permuted placebos as controls. Agreement was also described by emulation quality. Sensitivity analyses included all 38 emulated trials and CLMBR-T with 32 components. This study was exploratory, its analyses were specified after the results of our previous report were known, and all analyses are reported. Analyses were performed in Python 3.11 (eMethods 10).

## Results

### Trial selection and study population

The 32 analysed trials covered AF (12), diabetes (7), heart failure (5), hypertension (2), acute coronary syndromes or myocardial infarction (2) and other indications (4) (Table 1; Figure 1). Their cohorts comprised an aggregated 212,496 patients with both an ECG and a CLMBR-T representation (median, 4,729 per trial; range, 1,442–22,277). With PS-CVD5, 43,958 matched pairs were formed (median, 971 per trial; range, 314–4,100) [CHECK: computed from core/cells.csv; not stated in docs/v21], and on average 88.6% of the smaller treatment group was matched. Ten of the 32 trials were labelled physiologic: EAST-AFNET 4, CABANA, AFFIRM, AF-CHF, RAFT-AF, COMET, PARADIGM-HF, TRANSFORM-HF, EMPEROR-Preserved and LIFE.

### Covariate balance with PS-CVD5

Adding either learned representation to PS-CVD5 improved balance on the 54 held-out characteristics, and adding both improved it most (Figure 2A). The mean |SMD| decreased from 0.130 to 0.118 with the ECG (relative reduction, 8.8%; 95% CI, 4.4%–13.3%; 21 of 32 trials improved), to 0.114 with CLMBR-T (12.4%; 95% CI, 7.1%–17.7%; 23 of 32) and to 0.112 with both (13.5%; 95% CI, 6.6%–20.4%; 23 of 32). Each gain was robust to clustering by comparator (P ≤ .004) and remained significant after false discovery rate control across the whole sweep (q ≤ .003). The proportion of characteristics with an |SMD| <0.1 increased from 53.4% to 57.0%, 58.7% and 59.9%, respectively. The permuted placebos did not improve balance (ECG, 1.0%; 95% CI, −2.8% to 4.8%; CLMBR-T, 2.4%; 95% CI, −2.1% to 6.8%). On the 58-item panel, the relative reductions were 9.0%, 14.0% and 15.3%.

In the primary configuration, the two representations did not differ clearly in overall balance (CLMBR-T vs ECG, 3.9%; 95% CI, −1.8% to 8.9%; q = .36). Adding the ECG to PS-CVD5 + CLMBR-T reduced the mean |SMD| by a further 1.3% (95% CI, −4.2% to 6.6%; q = .49), and adding CLMBR-T to PS-CVD5 + ECG by 5.2% (95% CI, −1.5% to 11.6%; q = .18).

PS models that included CLMBR-T matched a smaller share of the smaller treatment group (79.1% with CLMBR-T and 77.5% with both, vs 88.6% with PS-CVD5 alone and 87.6% with the ECG). This restriction did not explain the CLMBR-T gain (eFigure 4). The per-trial gain was not correlated with the loss of retention (Spearman ρ = 0.21; P = .24). In the 15 trials in which CLMBR-T reduced retention by 5 percentage points or less, the relative reduction was 9.3% (95% CI, 2.0%–16.5%) with CLMBR-T and 6.9% (95% CI, 1.2%–12.7%) with the ECG, and with overlap weighting, which excludes no patients, it was 13.6% (95% CI, 8.8%–18.6%) with CLMBR-T. With 32 rather than 64 CLMBR-T components, the gain was 11.2% (95% CI, 6.5%–16.0%), and the 64-component permuted placebo gave none, so the gain was not an artefact of dimension (eFigure 5).

### Balance across the propensity score ladder

The added value of both representations was largest with the sparsest PS and diminished as hand-built covariates became richer (Figure 2A and 2B). With PS-Demo, the relative reductions were 11.3% (95% CI, 6.0%–16.1%) with the ECG, 20.7% (95% CI, 13.3%–27.8%) with CLMBR-T and 25.8% (95% CI, 19.0%–32.2%) with both [CHECK: core/sensitivity.csv; Figure 2A source, not stated in PAPER2_RESULTS.md], and CLMBR-T improved balance more than the ECG (10.5%; q = .022). With the hdPS, the ECG still improved balance (7.6%; 95% CI, 1.7%–13.0%; q = .033), whereas CLMBR-T did not (−3.5%; 95% CI, −14.0% to 6.0%), and at this rung CLMBR-T was worse than the ECG (−12.1%; q = .021). Neither representation improved balance when added to PS-Flags (ECG, −3.0% [CHECK: core/sensitivity.csv]; CLMBR-T, −2.9%; 95% CI, −8.7% to 3.0%) or to PS-Clinical (ECG, −1.3%; 95% CI, −6.8% to 3.9%; CLMBR-T, 0.8%; 95% CI, −3.9% to 5.9%). In the primary configuration, the balance contrasts that reached q < .05 were all within PS-Demo, PS-CVD5 or the hdPS; none involved PS-Flags or PS-Clinical, and no head-to-head across PS specifications did.

### What each representation captures

In the primary configuration, the two representations improved balance in different domains, but these differences were nominal and did not replicate across configurations (Figure 3A and 3B). With PS-CVD5, the ECG's gains were concentrated in the echocardiographic domains: LV function (16.4%; 95% CI, 2.5%–28.5%; q = .09), LV structure (14.9%; 95% CI, 3.5%–25.3%; q = .07) and diastolic function and left atrial size (16.1%; 95% CI, 6.1%–24.3%; q = .03), together with vital signs and core laboratory values (8.7%; 95% CI, 3.4%–13.8%; q = .02). CLMBR-T improved balance most in vital signs and core laboratory values (22.7%; 95% CI, 15.5%–29.4%) and other laboratory values (24.1%; 95% CI, 13.0%–33.2%) (both q < .01), and in valvular and aortic measures (15.9%; 95% CI, 4.5%–25.0%; q = .04), with little change in LV function (1.0%) or diastolic function (0.7%). Neither representation improved RV and pulmonary measures. In direct comparison, the ECG was nominally ahead of CLMBR-T for LV function (difference, −18.4%; q = .14) and diastolic function (−18.4%; q = .09) in the primary configuration, but not under 1:3 matching (−4.6% and −6.1%) or overlap weighting (−4.6% and −4.6%; all q ≥ .38). CLMBR-T was ahead for vital signs and other laboratory values in most configurations.

### Learned record representation vs hand-built covariates

There was no detectable difference in balance between PS-Demo + CLMBR-T and PS-Flags (Figure 3C). The mean |SMD| was 0.112 with PS-Demo + CLMBR-T and 0.113 with PS-Flags (relative reduction, 0.7%; 95% CI, −5.5% to 6.8%; 13 of 31 trials better; two-sided P = .81). The CI does not exclude differences of about 6% in either direction, so this is not evidence of equivalence. Adding CLMBR-T to PS-Flags did not improve balance (−2.9%; 95% CI, −8.7% to 3.0%), and CLMBR-T with 32 components gave the same result (−2.9%; 95% CI, −10.1% to 4.4%). Agreement with RCT results also did not differ (|Δ log HR| 0.186 with PS-Flags vs 0.174 with PS-Demo + CLMBR-T, paired over 31 trials; 19 of 31 closer; P = .50; benchmark-permutation P = .97).

With the common penalty, PS-Flags overfitted small trials. In PROTECT AF (1,442 patients and 622 flags), its in-sample C statistic was 1.00 and it formed fewer than 11 matched pairs, so that trial dropped out of the PS-Flags contrasts; across the other 31 trials, PS-Flags matched 73.5% of the smaller group on average, and cross-validation chose penalties 300 to 3,000 times stronger. The conclusion did not change with fairer specifications (Figure 3C; eFigure 4). Against a cross-validated PS-Flags, the relative reduction was −1.5% (95% CI, −7.5% to 4.4%; P = .63); with fixed penalties of 0.1 and 0.01, it was 7.7% (95% CI, −0.1% to 15.8%; P = .085) and 1.0% (95% CI, −5.1% to 6.8%); and in trials in which PS-Flags retained at least 50%, 70% or 90% of the smaller group (27, 19 and 11 trials), it was −1.1%, −2.6% and −5.1%. The point estimate therefore ranged from about −5% to +8% with the penalty or trial set, and no specification showed CLMBR-T balancing better than the flags at conventional significance. In one nominal sensitivity result, the cross-validated PS-Flags agreed less well with RCT results than PS-Demo + CLMBR-T (|Δ log HR| 0.216 vs 0.170; 21 of 32 trials; two-sided P = .069; benchmark-permutation P = .001); this did not hold with overlap weighting (P = .64; benchmark-permutation P = .70) or with the primary PS-Flags (benchmark-permutation P = .97).

CLMBR-T also did not outperform the hdPS or PS-Clinical (Figure 3C). Compared with the hdPS alone, the relative reduction was 3.8% (95% CI, −3.0% to 10.0%) for PS-Demo + CLMBR-T and 3.8% (95% CI, −2.7% to 9.6%) for PS-CVD5 + CLMBR-T. Compared with PS-Clinical, which includes measured vital signs, laboratory values and LVEF, PS-CVD5 + CLMBR-T was nominally worse (−4.9%; 95% CI, −11.2% to 0.9%; q = .25), significantly so only with the gradient-boosted PS (−11.9%; q = .007). Under IPTW, PS-Demo + CLMBR-T appeared better balanced than PS-Flags (28.0%; 95% CI, 14.0%–39.2%; q = .009) and the hdPS (17.9%; q = .004). This reflected extreme weights from near-separation of these rich PS models (median effective sample size fraction, 0.18 and 0.34, vs 0.72 with PS-CVD5), not a property of CLMBR-T.

### Agreement with RCT results

Both representations moved PS-CVD5 estimates closer to RCT results on average, but not toward each trial's own result (Figure 5B). The mean |Δ log HR| decreased from 0.226 to 0.196 with the ECG (20 of 32 trials closer; P = .049; cluster P = .20), to 0.166 with CLMBR-T (18 of 32; P = .030; cluster P = .046) and to 0.158 with both (18 of 32; P = .019; cluster P = .10). None survived false discovery rate control across the sweep (q = .58, .51 and .43), and none was trial-specific (benchmark-permutation P = .70, .93 and .94). The correlation with RCT estimates changed from 0.56 to 0.57, 0.50 and 0.59; estimate agreement increased from 15 to 22, 20 and 20 of 32 trials; and standardized difference agreement from 24 to 25, 26 and 30 of 32 trials. These agreement metrics improve when estimates move toward the null, and the benchmark-permutation results indicate that this attenuation, rather than trial-specific correction, accounts for the gains. The permuted placebos did not change agreement (0.226 and 0.227). In the prespecified confirmation in 5 AF trials, adding the ECG to PS-CVD5 worsened agreement (0.135 to 0.212; 1 of 5 trials closer) (eTable 9 of the paper 1 supplement).

### Replication across configurations

The balance gains with PS-CVD5 replicated with a caliper of 0.1, 1:3 matching and overlap weighting, but not uniformly with IPTW or the gradient-boosted PS (Figure 5A; eFigure 3). With the ECG, CLMBR-T and both, the relative reductions were 7.8%, 10.1% and 15.8% with a caliper of 0.1; 6.4%, 13.3% and 13.6% with 1:3 matching; and 7.4%, 13.6% and 16.2% with overlap weighting (all q ≤ .010). With IPTW, the ECG gain persisted (4.8%; 95% CI, 1.5%–8.5%; q = .013), but the CLMBR-T gains were lost (CLMBR-T, −2.0%; both, −0.9%); adding CLMBR-T reduced the median effective sample size fraction from 0.72 to 0.44. With the gradient-boosted PS, the ECG gain was lost (−1.1%; 95% CI, −7.3% to 4.3%), the CLMBR-T gain was borderline (6.9%; 95% CI, 1.1%–12.7%; q = .058) and the gain with both persisted (13.2%; 95% CI, 8.8%–17.9%; q < .001). The permuted ECG slightly worsened balance with overlap weighting and the gradient-boosted PS (q ≈ .05; two-sided). CLMBR-T improved overall balance more than the ECG with 1:3 matching (7.4%; q = .022), overlap weighting (6.7%; q = .012) and the gradient-boosted PS (8.1%; q = .036), but not in the primary configuration, with a caliper of 0.1 or with IPTW. Adding the ECG to PS-CVD5 + CLMBR-T improved balance with overlap weighting (3.0%; q = .005) and the gradient-boosted PS (6.7%; q = .009) but not in the other configurations.

Across the 337 contrast–configuration rows in the 32 trials, 98 reached q < .05 for the primary balance endpoint (18 in the primary configuration, 14 with a caliper of 0.1, 18 with 1:3 matching, 7 with IPTW, 23 with overlap weighting and 18 with the gradient-boosted PS), and no agreement row did, for |Δ log HR|, standardized difference agreement or the benchmark-permutation test (Figure 5C; eFigure 3). For the ECG, the agreement pattern of our previous report reproduced with PS-CVD5: 1:3 matching gave P = .007 (benchmark-permutation P = .037) and the gradient-boosted PS gave a benchmark-permutation P = .005, but neither survived false discovery rate control (q = .43 and .89), and no other configuration gave a trial-specific signal (benchmark-permutation P = .20 to .96). For CLMBR-T and for both representations, the benchmark-permutation P was at least .78 in every configuration. In all 38 emulated trials, the results were similar (PS-CVD5 + ECG, 9.0%; + CLMBR-T, 12.7%; + both, 14.5%; PS-Demo + CLMBR-T vs PS-Flags, 0.2%), and the only agreement rows with q < .05 were three isolated rows with the gradient-boosted PS (eFigure 5).

### Which trials benefit

The balance gains were nominally larger in the 10 trials labelled physiologic than in the 22 others, for both representations alike (Figure 4A and 4B). The relative reduction in physiologic vs other trials was 16.3% (95% CI, 7.8%–25.7%) vs 5.7% (95% CI, 1.0%–10.2%) with the ECG (label-permutation P = .048; q = .16), 22.6% (95% CI, 14.1%–31.0%) vs 8.0% (95% CI, 2.5%–13.6%) with CLMBR-T (P = .025; q = .13) and 29.6% (95% CI, 20.8%–38.3%) vs 6.7% (95% CI, −0.5% to 13.7%) with both (P = .004; q = .060). None of these differences survived false discovery rate control, and the placebos showed none (P ≥ .69). Gains in agreement did not differ by label (P ≥ .87). Because CLMBR-T, which is not a physiological signal, showed the same pattern as the ECG, larger gains in physiologic trials more likely reflect more confounding to remove in those trials than ECG-specific content.

One domain result survived false discovery rate control within the exploratory domain family (Figure 4C). In the 7 trials labelled for LV systolic function, the gain in mean |SMD| over the LV function characteristics was larger than in the other trials with the ECG (0.097 vs 0.011; P = .003), CLMBR-T (0.079 vs −0.018; P = .002) and both (0.122 vs −0.011; P = .001) (q = .021 for each), but not with the permuted ECG (P = .32). This result was weaker with the union of the two label sets (ECG, P = .005; q = .056; CLMBR-T, P = .037; q = .25), and it was shared by CLMBR-T, so it does not single out the ECG. No other domain label was associated with a larger gain at q < .05. Across trials, the gains correlated with the residual echocardiographic imbalance under PS-CVD5 (ECG, ρ = 0.36; P = .045; CLMBR-T, ρ = 0.41; P = .021), but so did the permuted CLMBR-T placebo (ρ = 0.46; P = .008), consistent with regression to the mean in trials with large and noisy residual imbalance; this check therefore cannot attribute the gains to the content of either representation.

By emulation quality, which was examined descriptively, adding the ECG to PS-CVD5 reduced |Δ log HR| from 0.216 to 0.171 in excellent or good emulations (9 of 15 trials closer; P = .078) and from 0.235 to 0.217 in moderate emulations (11 of 17; P = .21) (eTable 5 of the paper 1 supplement). The mean per-trial agreement gain with CLMBR-T was also larger in excellent or good than in moderate emulations (0.072 vs 0.049) [CHECK: Figure 5D source; not stated in docs/v21] (Figure 5D).

### Plasmode simulation

In plasmode simulations across 107 trial–confounder combinations in 31 trials, both representations removed part of the bias from a withheld physiological confounder when added to PS-CVD5, and neither approached adjustment for the confounder itself (eFigure 1). PS-CVD5 alone removed 18.6% of the bias, compared with 2.4% for PS-Demo, because the five diagnoses partly proxy the confounders. Of the bias remaining after PS-CVD5, the ECG removed a further 11.3% (Monte Carlo SE, 0.3), CLMBR-T 22.6% (0.3) and both 29.5% (0.4); the permuted CLMBR-T placebo removed 1.0% (0.4). The pattern differed by confounder. For LVEF, the ECG removed more than CLMBR-T (15.2% vs 8.4%), whereas for eGFR (2.5% vs 35.5%) and NT-proBNP (15.1% vs 28.4%), CLMBR-T removed more. This followed how well each representation predicted the confounder in the real data (cross-fitted R² for eGFR, 0.34 with CLMBR-T vs 0.05 with the ECG; for LVEF, 0.19 vs 0.22). CLMBR-T never sees the confounder's value, so its advantage for eGFR and NT-proBNP plausibly reflects codes for kidney and heart failure care and for which tests were ordered: legitimate proxy information, but coded-record information rather than physiology. The representations were partly complementary: added to PS-CVD5 + CLMBR-T, the ECG removed a further 8.9% overall and 9.7% for LVEF. The best arm, PS-CVD5 + ECG + CLMBR-T, removed 42.6% of the bias, vs 93.7% for adjustment for the confounder, and its CI coverage of the true effect was 76.9%, vs 94.4% for the oracle; every non-oracle arm had coverage between 51% and 77%.

### External replication in MIMIC-IV

In 7 cardiovascular trials in MIMIC-IV, adding the ECG to the PS-CVD5 analog reduced the mean |SMD| across the 26 held-out characteristics by 6.6% (95% CI, 2.3%–12.8%), with lower imbalance in 6 of 7 trials (P = .016); the proportion of balanced characteristics increased from 45.6% to 51.6%, with improvement in 3 of 7 trials (P = .125) (eFigure 2A). The permuted ECG did not improve balance (−3.1%). The size of the gain depended on design details: with any diabetes in place of type 2 diabetes, it was 11.8% (95% CI, 7.2%–16.7%), with index-admission codes included 11.5% (95% CI, 7.1%–17.4%), and after exclusion of index-day ECGs 6.5% (95% CI, −0.2% to 13.6%). The mean |Δ log HR| decreased from 0.344 to 0.326 with the ECG (4 of 7 trials closer; P = .27), but the permuted ECG moved it as much (0.323), so the change was not ECG-specific (eFigure 2C). In the negative-control trial PEPTIC (RCT relative risk, 1.05), PS-CVD5 gave an HR of 1.51 (95% CI, 1.35–1.69); adding the ECG moved the estimate toward the null (1.39; 95% CI, 1.24–1.55), whereas the permuted ECG did not (1.60).

## Discussion

In TRACE-ECG, we compared two learned representations, an AI-ECG embedding and CLMBR-T, a foundation model of the coded EHR, with hand-built PS covariates across 32 emulations of cardiovascular RCTs. Added to a PS of demographics and five cardiovascular diagnoses, each representation improved balance on held-out clinical, laboratory and echocardiographic characteristics, and the two together improved it most. These gains replicated across most matching and weighting approaches. They diminished as hand-built covariates became richer: CLMBR-T added nothing to a PS of yes/no diagnosis flags, the hdPS or PS-Clinical, and the ECG added to the hdPS but not to the diagnosis flags or PS-Clinical. Both representations moved estimates closer to RCT results on average, but this movement was attenuation toward the null rather than trial-specific correction, and no agreement result survived false discovery rate control in any configuration.

The two representations appear to capture different information, although the evidence for this is mainly indirect. In the primary configuration, the ECG's gains were concentrated in echocardiographic measures of cardiac structure and function, whereas CLMBR-T's were in laboratory values and vital signs; these domain differences were nominal and did not replicate under 1:3 matching or overlap weighting. The simulations gave a clearer picture: the ECG removed more bias from a withheld LVEF, and CLMBR-T more from withheld eGFR and NT-proBNP, in line with how well each predicted these quantities. CLMBR-T does not see measured values, but it does see diagnoses of kidney and heart disease and which tests were ordered, and these are informative proxies. This is coded-record information, which a sufficiently rich hand-built PS can also capture, and this is consistent with CLMBR-T adding nothing to PS-Flags or the hdPS. The ECG, by contrast, records physiology that is not coded, and it was the only representation that still improved balance with the hdPS.

The head-to-head comparison of PS-Demo + CLMBR-T with PS-Flags addressed whether a learned summary of the coded record adds information beyond the diagnoses themselves. A 64-component summary achieved about the same balance as several hundred diagnosis flags, and adding it to the flags did nothing. This held when PS-Flags was regularized by cross-validation and when the comparison was restricted to trials in which PS-Flags matched well, although the point estimate moved by several percentage points with these choices. The hypothesis that CLMBR-T captures the timing of diagnoses relative to the treatment decision, information that yes/no flags lack, was therefore not supported in this design. It was also not tested directly: CLMBR-T differs from PS-Flags in timing and in its access to drugs, procedures, measurement codes and visits, and we did not fit a PS with diagnosis dates. A practical reading is that CLMBR-T offers a compact, reusable summary that needs no feature engineering and performs about as well as a large hand-built flag set, but not better.

Agreement with RCT results did not distinguish the representations from attenuation. Each representation reduced the mean distance from RCT estimates and increased standardized difference agreement, up to 30 of 32 trials with both, but these metrics improve whenever estimates move toward the null, and reassigning RCT results across trials gave reductions as large. This reproduces the finding of our previous report and of earlier unpublished CLMBR-T analyses, and extends it across six configurations and 337 contrast–configuration rows. Balance on observed characteristics and agreement with RCTs are distinct targets; improving the first does not establish that the second will follow.

The trial labels gave a consistent but descriptive picture. Gains were nominally larger in trials in which treatment choice is plausibly driven by cardiac physiology, but the same was true of CLMBR-T, and a data-driven check could not separate this from regression to the mean. The only label result to survive false discovery rate control, larger gains in LV function characteristics in trials labelled for LV systolic function, was shared by both representations and rests on 7 labelled trials. These results are more consistent with representations helping where there is more confounding to remove than with the ECG specifically targeting physiological confounding.

Several limitations warrant consideration. This was an exploratory study. Its analyses were specified after the results of our previous report and of earlier CLMBR-T analyses were known, the 32-trial set was defined after the primary analyses of that report, and the primary balance panel, the number of CLMBR-T components and the primary trial set were proposed defaults [USER: confirm]. Better balance on observed characteristics does not establish exchangeability, particularly when those characteristics are measured selectively, and several held-out characteristics have coded proxies that PS-Flags, the hdPS and CLMBR-T can see. PS models with CLMBR-T matched about 10 percentage points less of the smaller treatment group, so balance was assessed in somewhat different populations; restriction did not explain the gains in our checks, but it changes the population to which estimates apply. The common PS penalty overfitted PS-Flags in small trials, and the PS-Flags comparison depended on the penalty by several percentage points. The trial labels came from two runs of one large language model that knows the published trials, the investigators had seen per-trial PS-CVD5 agreement results before the label analysis was designed, the groups were small, and no clinician has yet reviewed the labels. RCT estimates are imperfect benchmarks when populations, treatment implementation, follow-up or outcomes differ, and several emulations used placebo-proxy comparators. The simulations imposed selected confounding mechanisms with a post hoc orientation, did not include the hdPS, PS-Clinical or PS-Flags with CLMBR-T, and showed coverage below nominal for every arm without the confounder. Requiring both an ECG and a CLMBR-T representation restricts the population, the ECG could be recorded on the index day whereas CLMBR-T excluded it, and CLMBR-T was truncated to its most recent 4,096 tokens. Finally, the external replication included only 7 cardiovascular trials in an inpatient setting, without echocardiography or CLMBR-T, and its balance estimate moved by about 5 percentage points between near-identical specifications. While adding the PS-CVD5 analog, we found that the diagnosis-based PS of the three MIMIC-IV trials added in our previous report (SOAP II, ELITE II and PEPTIC) had included diagnoses from the index admission; with this corrected, the previously reported MIMIC-IV relative reductions with the ECG change slightly, from 7.5% (95% CI, −0.4% to 16.2%) to 8.1% (95% CI, −0.1% to 17.3%) with the hdPS and from 1.2% (95% CI, −9.1% to 11.8%) to 3.0% (95% CI, −8.6% to 15.2%) with PS-Clinical-lite, and the demographic PS results are unchanged.

In conclusion, learned representations of the ECG and of the coded EHR each improved measured balance when hand-built adjustment was limited to demographics and a few diagnoses, and they were partly complementary. CLMBR-T performed about as well as, but no better than, hand-built diagnosis flags, the hdPS or measured clinical covariates, whereas the ECG still added a small gain to the hdPS. Neither representation produced trial-specific agreement with RCT results. Learned representations may therefore be most useful as compact supplements to sparse adjustment, with their value set by information that the available structured covariates do not already contain.
