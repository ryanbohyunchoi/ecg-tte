**Background:** Trial emulation with real-world data is limited by unmeasured confounding. Propensity scores (PS) use prespecified structured variables that may miss latent features found in unstructured data. AI-ECG embeddings encode rich cardiac phenotypes that may capture them. We tested whether adding them to PS improves balance and agreement with randomized trials (RCTs).

**Methods:** Using Yale New Haven Health System records, we emulated 18 cardiovascular RCTs with new-user, active-comparator designs and 1:1 PS matching. PS used demographics and diagnoses (sparse PS) or high-dimensional codes (hdPS), with or without an ECG embedding. Balance was tested across 58 variables outside the sparse PS: medications, healthcare use, held-out codes, a prognostic score, 19 vitals and labs, and 35 echocardiographic measures. Hazard ratios were compared with RCTs by absolute log-HR difference and statistical consistency given both standard errors.

**Results:** Balance was similar with and without ECG (47 vs 48 of 58 variables with standardized difference <0.1). ECG moved sparse-PS estimates closer to the RCT in 14 of 18 trials. Mean absolute log-HR error fell from 0.18 to 0.16, statistical consistency rose from 61% to 78% (clinical PS 72%), and disagreement beyond sampling error fell 28%. The prespecified test was not significant. ECG added nothing to hdPS.

**Conclusion:** AI-ECG embeddings modestly improved RCT agreement for sparse PS without improving measured balance, suggesting they capture latent confounders.
